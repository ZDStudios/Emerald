The tab you forgot about

Every browser tab you opened three days ago and never closed is still holding memory. Most browsers pretend otherwise.

There is a particular kind of tab that nobody talks about. Not the one you are reading, and not the one you closed. The one from Tuesday — opened for a reason that made sense at the time, kept because closing it felt like admitting the reason had passed.

Browsers have a word for what they do with these. They call it discarding, or sleeping, or freezing, and the words are doing a lot of work. Sometimes the tab really is gone from memory and reloads when you return to it. Sometimes it has merely been told to stop running timers, and its several hundred megabytes are exactly where they were.

The difference is invisible from the tab strip, which is the only place most people ever look.

You can tell the two apart, but not from inside the browser. You have to open a process monitor and count. A tab that has genuinely been released takes its renderer process with it; a tab that has merely been throttled does not. One of these gives your machine back a gigabyte when you have twenty tabs open. The other gives you a slightly cooler laptop.

None of which is an argument for closing your tabs. It is an argument for a browser that tells you which kind of forgetting it is doing, and lets you set the threshold yourself, and does not treat the number of things you are holding in mind as a personal failing to be gamified away.
